01

The goal is to turn Hungary’s parliamentary transcripts into reliable structured data for longitudinal and correlational political research.

Source

A detailed record

The transcripts name the speaker of every speech and record interjections such as heckles and applause. Votes are recorded with exact counts and bills are numbered.

Problem

Published as PDFs

Party labels appear once, sometimes only in the table of contents. Votes are written as Hungarian prose, and interjections are set mid-paragraph in italics. No published quantitative study covers more than a handful of sessions.

Contribution

A reusable pipeline

It runs on any set of sessions. It is validated against hand-coding, and each output row records which layer produced it. Interpreting the results is left to future work.

02

Parsing a napló

The napló’s formatting rules are not documented, so I reverse-engineered them. Capitalised names open a turn, italic parentheses mark interjections, timestamps appear every ten minutes, and votes use four stock phrasings.

  1. The documentThe napló (országgyűlési napló) is the official PDF record of each sitting. Below is a short illustrative excerpt.
  2. What is extractedSpeakers and their parties, attacks, interruptions (applause, heckles, the chair’s bell), bill numbers and votes.
  3. The outputEach extracted item becomes one data row in the numbered list beside the excerpt.
  4. The toggle“As published” shows the original text. “As parsed” highlights each extracted item and fills in the rows.

03

Seven-stage pipeline

Each stage is a pure function with typed inputs and outputs, and can be tested or swapped on its own. Every figure is a deterministic function of one saved dataframe, so results replicate without rerunning the slow steps.

  1. 01
    PDF extractionPyMuPDF keeps font, CAPS, bold and italic flags. Italics are how interjections are found. pdf_io.py
  2. 02
    Table-of-contents parsingSpeaker → party map taken from the document’s own ToC. pdf_io.py
  3. 03
    SegmentationSpeeches, interjections, chair turns and votes; votes split on bill references and parsed from prose. parsing.py
  4. 04
    Rule-based scoringhuBERT sentiment + hand-curated accusative lexicon + direct-address markers → attack score and target. sentiment.py · analysis.py
  5. 05
    LLM refinementGemini 2.5 Flash-Lite scores each segment a second time; a seven-case merge protocol combines the two layers. llm_scoring.py · analysis_ai.py
  6. 06
    Per-session outputsCSVs, metadata.json and ~25 figures per sitting. data/processed/sessions/
  7. 07
    Longitudinal aggregationCombines every session CSV into 7 longitudinal tables and 15 figures. longitudinal.py

04

Merging rule-based and LLM scores

The rule-based layer is fast and auditable but scores sarcasm as praise. The LLM detects sarcasm but hallucinates targets and drifts between runs. The hybrid keeps rule scores for confident cases and uses the LLM to correct hard ones. A merge function with seven named outcomes decides each segment, and every row stores its merge_reason.

08

A proposed Parliamentary Climate Index

The index is a per-session score for how well parliament is functioning. It averages four components, each rescaled to [0, 1] across sessions. It is a prototype and has not been validated.

Cooperation

praise_x / (praise_x + attack_x)

Share of cross-bloc sentiment that is praise.

Consensus

mean |yes − no| / total

How lopsided the session’s recorded votes were.

Peace

1 / hostility

Inverse of mean cross-bloc attack intensity.

Order

1 / interjection rate

Inverse of interjections per speech.

09

Limitations and future work

Limitations

  • Transcription bias. The secretaries who write the napló are often MPs with party ties; selective transcription would carry into interjection rates.
  • Static bloc mapping. Government = Fidesz–KDNP holds after 2010 but not for the Antall or Horn eras; a date-indexed table is needed before pre-2010 claims.
  • Sarcasm. The pipeline catches roughly 60–70% of what a human reader flags; misses inflate the cooperation component.
  • Targets and procedure. Implicit or multi-party attacks resolve imperfectly; procedurally dressed attacks are zeroed.
  • Hungarian morphology. Case endings (Fidesz, Fideszt, Fidesznek, Fidesszel) and free compounding strain any lexicon.
  • Validation. One annotator, 126 segments. Indicative only; there is no formal inter-rater study.

Future work

  • Process the full corpus. ~1,800 sessions and 87,000 pages, released as a public research dataset.
  • Expand validation. Multiple fluent coders, inter-rater reliability, a sample stratified by year, party and session type.
  • Date-indexed blocs. A (start, end, governing parties) table so 1990–2010 can be read correctly.
  • Event windows. ±3-month comparisons around the 57 annotated events.
  • Controls and topics. PPP-adjusted GDP per capita and institutional indicators; topic models to split health, economy, foreign policy.
  • Refine the PCI. Test alternative weights against external expert assessments of parliamentary functioning.

Where this started

Hungary’s electoral incentives

An earlier, unfinished project asked how parties’ incentive strategies and changes to the electoral rules shaped party competition and voter behavior in Hungary from 1989 to 2025. I presented it as a work in progress at the Jackson School’s Academic Workshop Series.

Part of it was a first pass over about 56,000 pages of plenary transcripts with Python and an LLM API. That experiment became the pipeline on this page.

What it drew on

  • Archives. Library of the Hungarian Parliament, Hungarian National Archives, Central Statistical Office (KSH), Metropolitan Ervin Szabó Library.
  • Election data. Returns from the National Election Office, with KSH sociodemographics.
  • Party positions. Chapel Hill Expert Survey (1999–2019) and V-Party (2020–2025).
  • Literature. 61 scholarly papers, 32 reports and 5 datasets.
